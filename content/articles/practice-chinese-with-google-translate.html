You do not need a subscription to a language-learning app to make Chinese part of your day. Tools such as Duolingo can provide structure, but a computer or phone, a browser, and a short Chinese text are enough to begin a useful practice routine. From a teacher's perspective, the important question is what you do with the language. For the learner, the advantage is freedom: you can practise with topics you actually care about.

On a computer: translate a page or just a few words

On a computer, Chrome has built-in Google Translate. Open a Chinese webpage, select the translation icon beside the address bar, and choose your preferred language. You can also right-click the page to translate it. For a smaller hint, highlight a sentence, right-click, and translate the selection. See Google's Chrome instructions. Selecting just the unfamiliar words helps you keep reading the original Chinese without replacing the whole page with a translation.

Other browsers may use different services: for example, Edge uses Microsoft Translator. To use Google Translate from another browser, open translate.google.com, choose Chinese as the source language, and paste a short passage. No specialised learning app is required.

On a phone or tablet: make spare minutes useful

In Chrome on Android, open a Chinese webpage and use the translation prompt, or open the three-dot menu and tap Translate. In Chrome on an iPhone or iPad, you can also open the three-dot menu and choose Translate. Choose the language you want to read. Google's guides cover Android and iPhone and iPad.

For a single word or sentence, touch and hold the text, then adjust the selection handles. Use the translation option if your browser offers one. The menu can vary by device and version; you can always copy the text and paste it into Google Translate in your mobile browser. A short paragraph while waiting for a bus can become a small Chinese lesson.

Use the speaker button: hear Chinese as well as read it

A translation panel can do more than display a meaning. Look for a speaker button beside the Chinese text: where available, it plays spoken audio, sometimes labelled Listen or Speak. This narration lets you connect characters with their pronunciation. If your panel has no audio control, copy the text into Google Translate and use its speaker button. Google's text translation guide explains the listening controls.

Check which language you are playing. If Chinese is the original, listen on the Chinese source side; if you translated your own sentence into Chinese, listen on the Chinese result side. Listen once while reading, pause, and repeat aloud. Then look away and try again. Pay attention to tones and rhythm as well as individual sounds. The audio provides a model to imitate; it does not assess whether your own pronunciation is correct.

The teacher's perspective: think before you translate

Translation can support understanding, but understanding a translation is different from reading Chinese independently. First, look at the original sentence. Which characters do you recognise? Can you guess who is doing what? Then reveal the translation and check your prediction. Finally, return to the Chinese and read it again. This gives the tool a clear teaching role: it helps you check your thinking while keeping your attention on Chinese.

Turn unfamiliar words into practice with Wordcards

When you find a useful unfamiliar word, add it to your practice collection at wordcards.chinesewithbaiba.eu. Move from looking up a meaning to recalling it yourself. Choose a few words you expect to use, rather than saving every unknown character on a page. Keep the Chinese word, its pronunciation, meaning, and a short example together in your study notes so that the word retains its context.

For each card, try saying the word and recalling its meaning before revealing the answer. Then use it in a short sentence. From the teacher's perspective, this shows what you can retrieve independently. From the student's perspective, it gives each reading session a useful result: a small collection of words to practise again. Return to them tomorrow and later in the week.

The learner's perspective: a ten-minute routine

  1. Read for two minutes. Choose two or three lines from a recipe, travel page, or beginner dialogue. Pick something where you already recognise a few words.
  2. Check for two minutes. Translate one difficult sentence. Add a useful unfamiliar word to your Wordcards collection and note the sentence it came from.
  3. Listen for three minutes. Paste that sentence into Google Translate, keeping Chinese on the source side, and click or tap its speaker button. Listen, pause, and repeat aloud.
  4. Create for three minutes. Close the translation and use the phrase in a sentence about your own life. Review a saved wordcard, trying to recall the answer before revealing it.

For example, 我每天喝茶。 (Wǒ měitiān hē chá.) means “I drink tea every day.” Replace 茶 (chá, tea) with 水 (shuǐ, water): 我每天喝水。 Now you have used a sentence pattern yourself.

Keep your judgement involved

Treat machine translations as suggestions: context, politeness, and words with several meanings can produce misleading results. Translating a sentence back into English does not prove it is natural Chinese. Bring uncertain examples to your teacher, especially questions about tones and everyday usage. Measure progress by what you can understand and say without help. A small daily habit can give free tools a meaningful place in your Chinese learning.
